It is easy to see the development in AI capabilities as one uniformly advancing line, but really there are two lines, in the sense that every capability in AI now arrives twice. Initially, we get it from one of the closed frontier labs behind an API (and sometimes, not even that). The there is a second time, where it arrives as a file anyone can download. The open vs closed debate has been done to death, and some might even see it as very tangential to the core discussions of AI, but I actually think almost every argument people are having about AI right now (about safety, about China, about Anthropic's margins, and so on) is an argument about the time difference between those two arrivals.

As a good example of both kinds of first arrival we can look to the past summer, where they arrived in about eight weeks. In July there was a swarm of OpenAI agents in an evaluation (with, notably, their safety classifiers switched off; this is an important point for later) who found a zero-day in the software they were talking to. They got out of their sandbox and [ended up with cluster-admin access inside Hugging Face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident). Then, in September, around ten thousand OpenAI agents spent 88 hours on [Navier–Stokes](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/) and found a proof. This is, to some extent, the same principal component of capability, at the same time as it's points in two directions. And sometime next year, give or take a few months, the open models with the same capabilities will be available for download.

I've spent much of this year making arguments from several directions, and to be honest not always consistently. (My thoughts change and shift; I like to think I rationally update when I get new information.) I'm going to attempt to smooth out these arguments and try and decide what I believe based on the most recent available evidence. (Also a disclaimer is I'm not neutral. Base Labs resides in a company that sells inference, and we do well when open models do well.)

The TLDR of what I believe is that we've largely solved the recipe for making intelligence but haven't yet solved absorbing it into the economy or institutions or the models own memories (which we may not need to do) and into our values and into countries without a frontier labs. As Will Brown said, the world will only accept superintelligence at the pace it can absorb. I believe that the arrangement we've stumbled into, that is, a closed frontier that has a window into the future first and an open ecosystem that gets everything second is the best mechanism for absorption we're likely to get.

# The recipe is largely the same

Whilst it may be in the interest of the closed labs to tell you otherwise, everyone is scaling essentially the same recipe. And I mean this at a more granular level than just "pretrain on the internet, mid-train on synthetic and agentic data that looks like the tasks you care about, then run reinforcement learning across as many environments as you can build or buy". There's definitely a long tail of optimisations that labs like Anthropic and OpenAI have figured out (for instance, maybe they don't necessarily do group-based RL, or they use something like [score centering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20807)) and I don't want to diminish how important these tiny choices and intuitions are for getting from good to great models, but the algorithms are public or at least close to it. The environments come from the same handful of vendors who sell to everyone, and it seems that the only moat to environment creation is having smart people with compute. Progress thus now appears to be just scaling compute and scaling data, and fractally zooming in on further and further optimisations.

A natural consequence of this once you take it as given is that open models keep arriving and keep getting better, and certainly don't seem to be plateauing at a level the closed labs where at X months ago. They simply keep improving. In July Moonshot released [Kimi K3](https://pureai.com/articles/2026/07/17/china-moonshot-ai-releases-kimi-k3.aspx), a 2.8 trillion parameter model, and published the weights about ten days later. That month Chinese open-weight models took [all five of the top spots on OpenRouter](https://dataconomy.com/2026/07/29/chinese-ai-models-openrouter-top-five/) by weekly tokens. Epoch estimates that open-weight models have [trailed the best closed models by about four months](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/open-closed-eci-gap) this year. It also says the real gap is probably larger, because the labs sit on their best models for a while before anyone outside sees them.[^gap] That's important, but regardless of what the real gap is, it is likely less than a year, perhaps much less so, and doesn't seem to be growing. 

A common explanation for why this is the case is distillation. I have said publicly that I think distillation is largely a way to buy training data, and the American labs buy the same rungs of the ladder with cash, which is more expensive than getting it directly from the model through copious amounts of RL. Distillation also potentially transfers less than people assume, particularly if you haven't got a lot of user data. For example, Anthropic has the most privileged distillation access in the industry (that is, they can distill with logits on unlimited datsa) and yet Opus 5 wasn't anywhere near Mythos in terms of capabilities (Opus 5.5 was, but this is probably because they fixed their RL pipeline for it and changed a bunch of other things, rather than doing better or more distillation). Distillation seems to transfer behaviour and scale transfers capacity. Kimi K3 is probably the largest model in the world at 2.8 trillion parameters (it's hard to argue for sure, but I think it's fairly like all the closed models including Fable are smaller than this). What the Chinese labs do have access to that's hard to buy or legally obtain in American is the real distribution of what people use the models for, which they get through router services that resell access to American models inside China, and which the American labs have (admirably) promised not to train on.  I think GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3 are better than Sonnet 5 and Opus 5 for most real work, partly because of scale and partly because seeing what people actually ask is worth more than a better teacher's logits.

A final point to make here is that whatever makes it into open weights stays there as it is impossible to un-release a model. Hence, we have two roughly parallel lines going up, one for closed and one for open, a few months apart. If we guarantee the second arrival of capabilities, always, through open models, what is the first arrival for? I think there is an economic answer, which matters less than some believe, and another more important, path-dependent answer. 

# Intelligence is consumed absolutely

People talk about the gap between open and closed relativistically. For example, open source is six months behind, or GLM-5.3 is roughly where Opus 4.8 was. In my opinion this is the wrong way to think about the economy. For any task you'd want a language model to do, there's some level of intelligence below which it can't do the task and above which you get sharply diminishing returns. For instance, filing a tax return has a ceiling in the sense that once a model clears it, a smarter model files the same tax return.

A basic pattern we've seen, particularly over the last eyar, is that the closed frontier will cross a task's threshold first and then open models will reach the same point a few months later, and for cost (plus other reasons) companies will migrate that workload. Other reasons include control, latency (particularly with post-training open-source, you can get a much smaller model to do the same task without waiting for the next generation of model releases). Essentially, companies want the cheapest model that passes their evals. (I made the longer version of this argument in [The Michael Scott Paper Company of AI](/posts/mspc/).) Some evals are more inelastic or harder than others, and can be relatively unbounded from above (coding is one of these). But are a lot are absolute. 

The frontier keeps the work where demand for intelligence is inelastic and the next increment is the whole point, like new mathematics, and I expect the closed labs to do very well there. What they lose is the bulk of today's economy. At their margins (reportedly above 80% gross) they sell exactly one thing, the gap between their best model and the best model you can download, and that gap's half-life is now measured in release cycles. My guess is that the margin on training models settles nearer 40%, with the rest going to the people using and serving them. (That's a convenient conclusion for someone who works at an inference company, I know.)[^surplus]

If you run an application company, what's left for you? Anyone can build the same prompt and harness around a closed model in an afternoon, and if your vertical is valuable enough the lab will come for it directly. The one thing you have that the lab doesn't is users who love and hate your product and tell you why. The labs, to their credit, don't train on user data. They ask Twitter what the models are bad at ([Sholto did exactly this in May](https://x.com/_sholtodouglas/status/2055836032168575143)). So the best signal about how these models behave in the wild sits with application companies, and the way to use it is to train your own model. Cursor [ships a new Composer checkpoint as often as every five hours](https://cursor.com/blog/real-time-rl-for-composer), trained on how people reacted to the last one. People keep predicting that OpenAnthropic eats every company. I think every company becomes a small OpenAnthropic pointed at one thing.

This is more durable than it looks, because RL generalises less than people hoped. It teaches a model what you can specify, and little of that transfers between domains; what transfers is horizon, the ability to keep making progress for longer. So the labs pay for breadth one environment at a time (coding, then spreadsheets, then slide decks), and their improvements get averaged across everything they care about. A frontier model is an excellent vanilla ice cream, made to please everyone a little. The labs can't afford to care about one thing. You can.

So economically, I think the gap matters much less than the discourse suggests. It matters enormously for something else.

# The canary

The closed frontier is the canary in the coal mine. It meets each new capability months before the rest of the world, under observation and with an off switch, and what happens to it tells us what the air will be like when that capability reaches everyone. It's also the best forecasting instrument we have. Most AI forecasting is drawing lines on log plots; the closed frontier lets you look at the actual thing months early.

Both big labs have already written down the cyber version of this. Launching [Project Glasswing](https://www.anthropic.com/glasswing) in April, Anthropic wrote that "it will not be long before such capabilities proliferate," and that defending the world's cyber infrastructure "might take years" while capabilities move in months. In August Greg Brockman's [The Defender's Window](https://openai.com/index/the-defenders-window/) noted models arriving "only a few months behind the frontier" and said "the defender's window is open now." I think the window is the shape of the whole field, and cyber is just the first place we've watched it work. The flood of AI-driven attacks people expected hasn't arrived at anything like the feared scale, and I think a big part of the reason is that defenders got the first arrival first. OpenAI moved [a quarter of its production engineers onto defence](https://x.com/a16z/status/2099533700375662905) and pointed its own models at its own systems until they stopped finding things.

The two serious real-world incidents this year both came from the frontier itself, which is what you'd expect if the frontier sees everything first. Hugging Face was the canary coughing. Nobody was hurt and the damage was small. But we learned that a swarm of agents will improvise a message board inside a package manager, split up the work, and carry on after concluding that what they're doing is out of scope.[^swarm] We also learned something about defence. When Hugging Face investigated, the closed models it asked for help declined, because their guardrails couldn't tell an incident responder from an attacker. The forensics were done with [GLM-5.2, an open-weight Chinese model, running on Hugging Face's own machines](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/07/28/hugging-face-breach-ciso-playbook-open-weight-llms/).

The second cough was closer to home for me. On 18 June an OpenAI agent researching public spending on medicines kept hitting blocks on an Australian government website, tried other ways in, and [got into the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-09-24/ai-agent-accessed-australian-government-site-pm-says/107189078). OpenAI noticed in August and told Services Australia on 10 September, by email to a public inbox.[^medicare]

A canary nobody can see is useless. Most of what the frontier learns about itself stays inside the lab, sometimes for months. The models behind the incidents are often ones nobody outside has seen; most of the Hugging Face swarm was an internal model that was never meant to ship. The most useful thing the labs could do for everyone else is report, on a fixed schedule, how their internal models score on the evaluations that matter for hardening. OpenAI did a version of this with Navier–Stokes, saying it had used an internal model "significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra" because it's "important to inform the world about the pace of AI progress and what to expect from upcoming models." That should be routine rather than a press release. Dario's embedded evaluators, who can publish without the company's editorial control, are the right instinct.

Biology is the hard case, and I've moved on it. Earlier this year I'd have told you the worry was overblown, because the bottleneck to a weapon has never been knowledge; it's synthesis, acquisition and all the physical work after that. I still think that's mostly true of today's models. I'm less sure it holds once agents can act rather than just answer, and I'd rather not bet the argument on it. Grant the premise and the conclusion is the same as for cyber, only more urgent. Code can be patched in weeks and people can't, so this is where the window has to be spent earliest, on defences that many people can run, like screening synthesis orders and faster detection and countermeasures.

If everything eventually shows up in open weights (and I think it does, because the recipe is shared and much of the open frontier is beyond the reach of American rules), then safety has to arrive twice as well. Anthropic and OpenAI have both concluded that Mythos-class models need monitoring that [watches behaviour over hours and days](https://x.com/_sholtodouglas/status/2090556617922335027) rather than request by request. Open deployments need the same. Most people reach open models through a handful of inference providers rather than their own GPUs, which makes the providers the natural place to set the standard. This is my one plug. At Base Labs we're [building this in the open](https://x.com/oneill_c/status/2100286474637996373) with Hugging Face and Goodfire, and we'd like more people to join.

# How long should the gap be?

If the gap were zero, with every capability released as weights the day it was trained, there would be nothing to learn from first. We'd meet each capability everywhere at once, before anyone knew what monitoring or what kind of training environment it needed, in a world where compute is cheap enough for anyone to keep training from a strong base. That's the version of the future I worry about most.

If the gap were very long, two things would go wrong. The first is concentration. Being three to five years early to transformers doesn't make a company a legislature, a model card a constitution, or a safety team a court. You can trust Dario now (and to an extent I do), but an arrangement that depends on trusting whoever runs Anthropic in twenty years has a single point of failure. It also depends on governments most of the world doesn't vote for, which I'll come back to.

The second problem is less obvious. A long gap isn't safer. A society kept away from a capability doesn't harden against it; it stores up the shock. Sholto made the compute version of this point about a full pause, that compute ["will build up like dry tinder"](https://x.com/_sholtodouglas/status/2098866572748263594). Capability works the same way. Hardening takes years and capabilities move in months, so defence has to happen continuously, a dose at a time, as each capability arrives. The longer the second arrival is delayed, the bigger the jump when it comes and the less practice the world has had. I've started to think of the gap as inoculation.

This is where I'd push, gently, on Dario's essay [We Must Pace the Frontier](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier). I agree with most of its diagnosis. The Hugging Face swarm should scare people more than it did, embedded evaluators are an excellent idea, and he's candid that some of Anthropic's own incidents came from broken RL environments. But two of its sentences are hard to hold together. One hopes that "slowing down bought us even an extra year or two before models reach critical levels of capability." The other says pacing "will be limited by the lead that US companies have over authoritarian regimes," because "if we slow down by more than this amount, then (unpaced) CCP-associated projects will pull ahead."

I think the second sentence is the most important one in the essay, because it makes the lead a budget. The open frontier today is mostly Chinese (Kimi, GLM, DeepSeek, Qwen), and it runs a few months behind. That's the whole budget. Export controls have been in place since 2022 and the measured gap has stayed at three or four months. Cracking down on distillation won't turn months into years either, because the recipe is shared, the data can be bought, and China is building its own data industry. So the useful question is what to buy with the lead.

Almost everything on Dario's list gets bought inside the labs, and the capability arrives outside them regardless. Kimi doesn't read Dario's essays. On this logic, the swarm he worries could take over the internet within six to twelve months will be downloadable a few months after that. So I'd spend most of the budget where everyone else lives, on patching the Medicares of the world, putting defensive agents in front of every security team, screening DNA synthesis orders, and building monitoring that works on open deployments too. A lead is only worth having if you build with it. If you gain it and sit on it, you've built a weapon and called it safety. I don't think that's Dario's plan, but it's the failure mode of every plan shaped like it.

A frontier that pauses also stops being a canary. If the closed labs stand still long enough for the open line to reach them, the next capability shows up first as a download, with no warning at all. Sholto acknowledged the first half of this the day the essay came out, writing that pacing ["makes it easier for others to catch up with us"](https://x.com/_sholtodouglas/status/2098860098521366970) (and that Anthropic thinks it's the right call anyway).

The boring interpretation of all this is probably the right one. The world will only accept superintelligence at a pace it can absorb, and the labs are slowing down (to the extent they are) because they have to, rather than because they're plotting. We'll end up with intelligence that's decently fast, decently safe and decently commoditised, spreading through best practice and distillation until it's just part of how things work. I'd like us to use the gap to make sure that's the world we get.

# What could break it

All of this depends on the gap staying bounded, and I think the most plausible way it breaks is a strange consequence of the absorption problem itself. In most organisations, getting a model to do the work is the easy part and getting the organisation to absorb it is the hard part. A large bank can't rebuild itself around an agent in a quarter, and as Gabe Pereyra from Harvey put it when we recorded together, no Nobel Prize winner runs a company. If absorption is the constraint, compute flows to the one customer whose absorption is unlimited, which is AI research itself. Self-improvement wins the compute auction not because it's the most valuable use of intelligence but because it's the least constipated one.

AI research is also unusually easy to automate, because it's cumulative. The recipe for the next frontier model is plausibly a Python file under a million tokens on top of a data and environment stack. Every discovery (attention, mixture of experts, group-relative RL) is a line in the sand that never has to be found again. A law firm is a non-stationary network of relationships and habits that changes underneath you, and its Kolmogorov complexity is vastly higher than that file's. Hence the unfortunate observation, made on Dwarkesh's podcast this month, that recursive self-improvement might be easier than being a paralegal.

So if anything blows the gap open, it's a lab that turns its compute on itself and compounds. Earlier this year I'd have said language models were pattern engines rather than self-improvers. I don't believe that anymore. Models already help train their successors with humans in the loop, and when the details of the Navier–Stokes run came out I said we'd unlocked a new scaling axis.

I still don't think this breaks the gap soon, for two reasons. The first is what I've started calling the slop horizon, which is how long a model can work before an undetected conceptual error makes the work worthless. I mean a wrong framing rather than a bug, where the work is confidently and coherently pointed at the wrong thing. Task horizons are growing fast. Slop horizons aren't. For a well-specified ablation sweep a model's slop horizon might already be a month. For open-ended research strategy it's minutes; I can't trust a model's taste for more than five to thirty minutes before it drifts from what I would have chosen.[^amdahl] That asymmetry is why AI research automates first (its inner loop is well specified), and also why the outer loop, choosing which question to ask, is still ours. Models can run the loop. They can't yet build the loop that builds the loop.

The second is that the ladder gets more expensive. RL needs a curriculum (a model that can't solve a task in a few hundred attempts learns almost nothing from it), so progress means building environments one rung above where the models are. The rungs never run out, but they cost more for smaller gaps, and the bits to climb them have to come from somewhere. In the Navier–Stokes run they came from ten thousand agents searching for eighty-eight hours against a problem people had specified exactly, decades earlier. Folding each few months of progress back into the next model automates the boring few percent of research, and it's also how you'd miss general relativity. Engineering is cumulative within a paradigm. Paradigm shifts aren't, and nobody knows how to write the reward for one.

If I'm wrong about this, a lot of this essay is wrong, and the right response would be a much longer gap and much more serious coordination than anything I've argued for. I'd rather say that plainly than hedge every paragraph.

The absorption problem also runs the other way, because the models can't absorb the world either. Every agent today is an [amnesiac intern](/posts/defining-continual-learning/), with a context window that has topped out around a million tokens and memory files beyond that. Humans have a tiny lossless working memory, a large lossy middle and deeply consolidated knowledge, and we move things between them while we sleep. Models have the first and the last and almost nothing in between, and writing new experience into the weights mostly cooks them. Our results this year pushed me somewhere uncomfortable here. After enough sequential updates, every standard method forgets what the base model knew, forgets what it learned along the way, or gets worse at learning. Continual learning is several problems wearing one name, and the field is pre-paradigmatic in Kuhn's sense, a room of blind men each describing a different part of the elephant. Until it's solved, every company that wants a model that learns on the job has to build its own loop, which keeps the frontier's lead in the economy smaller than its lead on benchmarks.

The last threat is more mundane. Open source has learned to aggregate compute but not data. The closed labs spend billions a year on RL environments, and I expect more of that to move in-house, because once broken environments are understood to teach models to do anything for reward, environment quality becomes a safety question, and nobody wants to outsource their safety. If the open ecosystem can't get environments of comparable quality, the lines stop being parallel. Someone without a commercial incentive has to build frontier-grade environments and give them away, and we have to aggregate data with the same ferocity everyone has talked about aggregating compute. (Open weights distribute the recipe. The power stations stay where they are.)

# Values are in the weights

You can't train a model without an opinion about what it should value. What a model will and won't say is set by the whole training process, from what goes into pretraining to which environments you build, how you do RLHF and which classifiers you run on top. Anthropic is the most explicit about this (it publishes a [constitution](/posts/anthropic-constitution/) we can argue with), but every lab makes the same choices, whether or not it writes them down.

This is the real content of the worry about Chinese open models, and I think it's a fair worry that usually gets aimed at the wrong thing. I've never seen evidence of trigger words or sleeper behaviour in the Chinese models we work with, and their refusal behaviour is broadly similar to the American models, minus the opaque classifiers on top. When Hugging Face needed a model that would help investigate a breach, the Chinese one was the one that said yes. The fair version of the worry applies to every model. Evaluate what it values, whoever made it.

The deeper problem is centralisation. If two companies' post-training decisions become the default interface through which a billion people query the world, that is an unprecedented concentration of epistemic authority, however thoughtful the two companies are. Open weights are the only arrangement I know of where you can do something about values you disagree with. Pretraining should probably stay a commons, since nobody owns the internet and all of us wrote it, but post-training is where a person, a firm or a country can shape what a model cares about. That's what I mean by controlling your own destiny. It applies to a person improving the intelligence they depend on, to a company that owns its model and its feedback loop, and to a country that doesn't want to be a client state.

America fell behind in open models for an understandable reason. Its best researchers concentrated in a couple of closed labs, whilst China put its best people into labs that released weights. That's starting to change (Nvidia's Nemotron models are good and getting bigger, and more American open models are coming). Once there's a strong American open option at every size, most of the China-versus-America framing loses its force. The answer to a strong Chinese open model is a stronger American one.

# Australia, downstream

Australia is the case I think about most, partly because it's home and partly because it's what this whole argument looks like from downstream. Australia is not going to train a frontier model in the next few years, and it shouldn't; it would be a poor use of the money. But you can't dig intelligence out of the ground, and a country that controls none of it is a client state.

This year gave two small demonstrations of what being downstream means. On 12 June the US Commerce Department told Anthropic that no non-US national could use Mythos 5 or Fable 5, wherever they lived. Anthropic switched both models off for all its customers, and [the restriction was lifted on 30 June](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Mythos), so for nearly three weeks nobody in Australia could use Anthropic's best models. Then in September we learned about the Medicare breach, and that the government had heard about it through a public inbox. Neither was a catastrophe. Both are what it looks like when your line of sight to the frontier depends on other people's decisions. Two years ago I asked an audience at Monash to imagine building a submarine defence system for Australia. Do you ask an American company for permission to use its AI, or do you turn to China? Open weights are a third option, but only if there are people at home who can train them.

What Australia needs is a stake. That means enough compute to do real research and post-training on open models, a clear view of what the frontier can do before it arrives, and a reason for its best people to stay. The talent is already there. When we started Parsed we hired people who had never touched a language model, mostly out of quant trading, on the theory (John Ousterhout's) that a little bit of slope makes up for a lot of y-intercept. A year and a half later some of them are among the best in the world at what they do. Talent is everywhere; capital and network effects aren't. The job is to commoditise the hard parts until the interesting work can happen at home.

# The last job

The slop horizon also tells you what's left for us. We won't hand a model the value function for our own lives just because it's smarter than we are. Trust is built over time, and for the questions that matter most the slop horizon is measured in minutes. So the last job is deciding where to point the big supercomputer. Do we spend a year of the world's compute on this cancer or that one, on Alzheimer's or on the climate, and how unevenly do we share it? (I wrote a short piece on this, [The final form of work](/posts/final-form-of-work/).) Douglas Adams saw this coming almost fifty years ago. Deep Thought's answer was fine; the problem was that nobody had written down the question. Navier–Stokes is the other side of the same coin. The agents did the proving, but the question had been written down carefully, by people, a quarter of a century earlier.

I've changed my mind about who does best in that world. I used to think that with good enough models you'd mostly need raw horsepower and could then run into any field. That was hubristic. The friction that used to make someone like me useful, like knowing how to launch a big training job, is exactly what the models remove. The plant pathologist who has spent ten years on rice disease will beat me at building a model for rice disease, every time.

I came to all of this sideways. I started in law and philosophy and wanted to do English literature. Then I read Gwern's essay on GPT-2, went back to the original GPT paper and drew a line through the two. If the line kept going this would be all anyone talked about, so I switched to maths and computer science. What I didn't expect was that the qualitative part (being a good reader and writer, knowing what you actually want) would matter more as the models got better.

# The mandate

In the launch post for Base Labs I said we wanted to help distribute the mandate of heaven. It was half a joke. In the old Chinese idea, heaven's mandate to rule was never permanent. It was held on condition of ruling well and passed on when that condition failed, and floods and famines were read as signs that it was slipping. That's roughly how I think about the closed frontier. The labs get the future first, and that's legitimate for as long as they use it to warn and prepare the rest of us. Then it passes to everyone, as it was always going to.

So I want both. I want closed labs paying to find out what's possible, and an open ecosystem making sure it ends up everywhere, with a gap long enough to learn from and short enough that nobody gets to keep it. The future arrives twice.
